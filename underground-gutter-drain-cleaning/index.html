What an underground gutter drain is

On many Houston homes the downspout does not end at a splash block. It drops into an adapter at ground level and feeds a buried 3- or 4-inch pipe that carries roof water away from the slab. That pipe ends at one of three places: an outlet through the curb at the street, a pop-up emitter in the lawn that lifts open when water pushes up from below, or a low corner of the yard or a side ditch.

You see these lines most on newer suburban builds and on older homes where a remodel tied the downspouts in to keep water off a walkway or patio. They do a good job of protecting the foundation, right up until they clog. Because the pipe is out of sight, the first sign is usually a problem somewhere else: an overflowing gutter, a soggy flower bed or water standing against the slab.

Two kinds of pipe are common. Black corrugated pipe is cheap and flexible, but its ridges catch grit and it can be crushed by a vehicle tire or a settling bed. Smooth white PVC holds its shape and flushes cleaner, though its glued or push-fit joints can still pull apart when the clay around them moves.

Signs your buried drain line is clogged

  • One corner of the gutter overflows in a hard rain, even though the trough was just cleaned.
  • Water spills out at the bottom of the downspout, where it meets the buried adapter, instead of going down.
  • The pop-up emitter never opens, or opens only with a trickle long after the rain started.
  • The curb outlet stays dry during a storm, or leaves a fan of mud on the street afterward.
  • A strip of lawn along the pipe route stays soft for days, which can mean water is leaking out of a broken section.
  • Water stands next to the foundation near a downspout after every rain.

Why buried drain lines clog on Houston lots

Very little fall. Much of Houston is close to flat, so a line running 30 or 40 feet to the street may drop only a few inches. Slow water drops whatever it carries, and silt settles in the low spots.

Roof grit and clay silt. Asphalt shingles shed sand-like granules every rain, and they are heavy enough to sink and pack. Where the adapter at ground level is loose, clay soil washes in with them and sets up like mortar.

The spring live oak shed. Live oaks are not true evergreens; Texas A&M AgriLife’s Plant Clinic notes they drop their old leaves as new ones emerge in spring. Small leaves and catkin strings wash straight through a gutter and into the downspout, where they mat together in the first bend underground.

Roots at the joints. Tree roots grow toward moisture. A corrugated joint or a cracked fitting that seeps a little water is an open invitation, and once fine roots get inside they catch everything that flows past.

Clay that moves. Houston’s expansive clay swells when wet and shrinks when dry. Over a few seasons that movement can put a sag, or belly, in a buried pipe. Water and silt sit in the belly between rains, and the line slowly fills from the low point outward.

Flushing, snaking or a camera: what each one does

Flushing is where every underground gutter drain cleaning starts. Water is run into the line from the downspout end to push loose silt, grit and leaves through to the outlet, and the outlet or emitter is checked to confirm water is leaving. It clears most clogs, because most clogs are loose debris. This is the part we handle with every gutter cleaning, and we confirm the price first if a line turns out to be packed.

Snaking and jetting use a cable or high-pressure water to cut through roots or packed material. Camera inspection runs a small camera down the pipe to find a crushed section, a separated joint or a belly. Those are drainage and plumbing tools. If a line still backs up after flushing, roots or damaged pipe are the likely cause, and the honest next step is a drainage or plumbing contractor, not another flush.

Gutter cleaner or drainage pro: who to call

  • Call a gutter cleaner when the gutter overflows, the downspout is slow, or the emitter stopped opening after a leaf season. Those are usually debris problems that cleaning and flushing fix.
  • Call a drainage or plumbing contractor when a line still backs up after it has been flushed, when you can see a sunken or soggy strip along the pipe route, or when roots are coming out of the outlet.
  • Call a structural engineer if you already see foundation cracks, sticking doors or gaps at the trim. A drain line can contribute to those, but it does not diagnose them.

Checks you can do between cleanings

Put a garden hose in the top of each downspout that goes underground and let it run for a few minutes while someone watches the outlet. Water should show up at the curb or lift the emitter. If it backs up at the adapter instead, the line needs attention. Keep mulch, grass and soil pulled back from emitter lids so they can open, and look at the curb outlet after the first big storm of the season. Skip the pressure washer: a strong jet can split corrugated pipe or blow a joint apart.

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my underground downspout drain is clogged?

Run a garden hose into the top of the downspout for a few minutes. If water backs up and spills out at the joint where the downspout meets the buried pipe, or the outlet at the curb or the pop-up emitter never runs, the line is blocked somewhere between the two. Overflow at one corner of the gutter during heavy rain, even right after a cleaning, is the other common sign.

Can a buried gutter drain be cleaned without digging it up?

Often, yes. Most clogs are silt, roof grit and leaves, and flushing from the downspout end moves them through to the outlet. Digging only comes into it when the pipe is crushed, separated at a joint or full of roots, and that work belongs to a drainage or plumbing contractor.

Why does water come out of the bottom of my downspout when it rains?

The buried line below it cannot take the water as fast as the roof sends it, so it backs up and escapes at the first loose joint, usually the adapter at ground level. That points to a clog or a sag in the underground pipe rather than a problem with the gutter itself.
